# God-Man or God’s Man? Church-Traditional Trinitarianism vs. the Biblical Unitarian View of Jesus Christ in the Context of Hebrew Intertestamental Wisdom Literature Christology and the Jewish Law of Agency During Second Temple Judaism

## I. Introduction: God-Man or God’s Man?

The question posed by the contrast “God-Man or God’s Man?” is not whether the New Testament gives Jesus Christ an extraordinarily elevated status; it unquestionably does. The real question is what kind of elevation the New Testament intends. Does it identify Jesus ontologically as the one God of Israel existing eternally as a second divine person within a tri-personal Godhead, as later Nicene Christianity came to confess, or does it identify him as God’s uniquely begotten, authorized, Spirit-anointed, resurrected, and exalted human Messiah who represents God so perfectly that the titles, actions, authority, honor, and even language of God can be attributed to him representationally? This study argues for the latter interpretation. Its thesis is that the Biblical Unitarian reading better explains the total pattern of biblical evidence when that evidence is read against Israel’s scriptural categories, Second Temple Jewish wisdom traditions, Jewish concepts of commissioned agency, messianic kingship, prophetic representation, and the New Testament’s repeated distinction between “God” and “Jesus Christ.” This does not require minimizing Jesus. On the contrary, it requires taking seriously the astonishing biblical claim that God has appointed a genuine human being to be his definitive image, Messiah, Son, mediator, judge, revealer, ruler, savior, and Lord.

## II. Methodological Foundations: Reading Jesus Within His Jewish World

The argument is not that every verse traditionally used for Christ’s deity is easy to reinterpret, nor that all Trinitarian exegesis is linguistically impossible. Several passages genuinely constitute difficult counterevidence and must be acknowledged as such. Historical and theological interpretation is not mathematical demonstration; what can be established is comparative explanatory power. The question therefore becomes which model explains the greatest amount of evidence with the fewest imported assumptions. It is significant that what is now called classical Trinitarianism emerged through a centuries-long process of theological clarification and controversy. The fully developed doctrine—that the one God eternally exists as three equally divine persons sharing one essence—was not simply quoted from a biblical sentence. The recognizably mature pro-Nicene formulation crystallized particularly through the fourth-century controversies surrounding Nicaea and Constantinople. Nicaea in 325 confessed the Son as *homoousios*, “of the same essence,” with the Father, while the theological meaning of that confession was elaborated throughout the subsequent fourth-century controversies. This historical fact does not by itself disprove Trinitarianism, because a doctrine may theoretically be a legitimate later inference from earlier revelation, but it means one cannot simply assume the Nicene metaphysical framework while asking what a first-century Jewish author naturally meant.

## III. A Necessary Historical Disclaimer: Second Temple Judaism Was Not Monolithic

Second Temple Judaism was not a single, uniform theological system. One should not imagine that every Jew possessed an identical theology of angels, Wisdom, the Messiah, resurrection, divine agency, heavenly intermediaries, or eschatological figures. Jewish literature of the period exhibits substantial diversity. Some texts speak dramatically of personified Wisdom; Philo of Alexandria employs Logos language so elevated that he can speak of the Logos as God’s image, firstborn, cosmic mediator, and in certain contexts extraordinarily “divine,” while still distinguishing the supreme God from the mediating Logos. Other writings envision exalted angels or heavenly humanlike figures, while portions of 1 Enoch portray a Son of Man or Chosen One associated with heavenly preexistence and eschatological judgment. This is an important counterargument to any simplistic claim that “Jews could never imagine a preexistent Messiah.” Some Jewish traditions clearly could employ such concepts. Yet this diversity strengthens rather than weakens the need for precision. Preexistence, divine agency, heavenly status, personification, participation in divine prerogatives, and ontological identity with Israel’s God are not interchangeable concepts. A figure can be heavenly without being YHWH, preexistent without being coequal with God, called divine without sharing God’s essence, or serve as God’s visible representative without being the God whom he represents.

## IV. Divine Agency in the Hebrew Bible: The Scriptural Foundation

The Hebrew Bible establishes the grammar of representation long before the New Testament. Israel’s God repeatedly authorizes agents to speak and act with an authority so complete that the actions and speech of the agent can be narrated as God’s own. This phenomenon is foundational for understanding later Jewish concepts of agency and provides a conceptual environment in which an agent can bear extraordinary divine authority without becoming ontologically identical with the sender.

## V. Moses as “God”: Functional Divinity and Representative Authority

Moses provides the clearest human example. In Exodus 4:16 Aaron is to function as Moses’ mouth while Moses will be “as God” to Aaron, and in Exodus 7:1 YHWH tells Moses, “I have made you God [*elohim*] to Pharaoh,” with Aaron functioning as his prophet. Moses does not become an ontological deity. Rather, the representational chain is God to Moses to Aaron. What God is to Moses, Moses becomes functionally to Aaron and Pharaoh. Moses bears divine authority because God commissions him. This establishes an indispensable linguistic principle: being called *elohim* does not automatically identify the recipient as the one supreme God of Israel.

## VI. The Angel of YHWH: When the Messenger Speaks as the Sender

The phenomenon becomes still clearer in biblical messenger narratives. In Genesis 16 the angel of YHWH speaks with first-person divine authority, promising to multiply Hagar’s descendants, after which Hagar identifies the encounter with the God who sees her. In Genesis 22 the angel of YHWH says to Abraham, “you have not withheld your son … from me,” although the sacrifice is obviously understood as an act of obedience to God. In Exodus 3 the angel of YHWH appears in the burning bush, yet the ensuing speaker identifies himself as the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. In Judges 2 the angel announces, “I brought you up from Egypt,” an act elsewhere attributed directly to YHWH. Exodus 23:20–21 supplies an interpretive key when God promises to send an angel before Israel and warns them to obey him because “my name is in him.” A commissioned messenger may therefore possess God’s name, exercise divine prerogatives, and alternate linguistically with God without eliminating the conceptual distinction between sender and messenger.

## VII. Kings, Judges, and Divine Representatives in Israel

Human kings and rulers furnish another analogy. Psalm 45 is especially important because Hebrews 1 later applies it to Jesus. The royal figure can plausibly be addressed as *elohim*, “God” or “god,” while the very next verse says, “therefore God, your God, has anointed you.” Whatever translation one prefers, the poem unmistakably places the royal figure beneath the God who anoints him. Psalm 82 likewise uses *elohim* for beings subordinate to the Most High. Whether one interprets these figures as human rulers, celestial members of a divine council, or some convergence of the two, the decisive lexical point remains that *elohim* does not invariably designate the one supreme God. Similarly, 1 Chronicles 29:23 says Solomon sat “on the throne of YHWH” as king. No Israelite would infer that Solomon literally became YHWH because he occupied YHWH’s throne. The king’s throne is God’s throne because the king governs as God’s royal representative. Judges raised by God can function as “saviors” even though YHWH is Israel’s ultimate Savior; prophets speak God’s words; priests pronounce divine blessing; kings administer God’s judgment. Scripture repeatedly distinguishes the ultimate source of authority from the human or heavenly agent through whom that authority operates.

## VIII. The Jewish Law of Agency and the *Shaliaḥ* Principle

The later rabbinic maxim *sheluho shel adam kemoto*, commonly summarized as “a person’s agent is as himself,” explicitly formulates a representational logic whose practical roots are visible much earlier in Scripture. In Jewish legal agency, an authorized representative could perform actions legally attributed to the principal who commissioned him. The representative remained numerically distinct from the sender while acting with the sender’s authority.

## IX. Historical Caution: Rabbinic Agency and the First-Century Context

A necessary historical disclaimer must be maintained. The Babylonian Talmud is later than the New Testament, and one must not commit an anachronism by pretending that a fully codified rabbinic doctrine found in later texts can simply be projected unchanged into the ministry of Jesus. The safer historical conclusion is not that Jesus consciously followed a later rabbinic handbook of *shaliaḥ* jurisprudence, but that the later maxim crystallizes a much older biblical and Jewish representational pattern. The sender acts through the sent one; receiving the authorized representative can count as receiving the sender; rejecting the messenger can count as rejecting the principal.

## X. Jesus as the Supreme Agent of God

Jesus himself articulates precisely this logic. “Whoever receives you receives me, and whoever receives me receives him who sent me” in Matthew 10:40 creates a chain of representation. Luke 10:16 states that rejecting Jesus’ messengers constitutes rejection of Jesus and ultimately rejection of the one who sent Jesus. John 13:20 likewise says that whoever receives one whom Jesus sends receives Jesus, while whoever receives Jesus receives the one who sent him. The implications are profound. The disciples can represent Jesus so fully that receiving them is receiving Jesus, yet no one concludes that the disciples are ontologically identical with Christ. The same representational logic must therefore be considered when Jesus says that receiving or seeing him is receiving or seeing the Father.

## XI. Johannine Agency Christology: “The One Who Sent Me”

This agency background becomes especially powerful in the Gospel of John, paradoxically the Gospel most frequently invoked for Christ’s ontological deity. John repeatedly defines Jesus as the one sent by another. Jesus says his food is to accomplish the work of the one who sent him in John 4:34; the Son “can do nothing from himself” in John 5:19; the Father has given the Son authority to execute judgment in 5:27; Jesus seeks not his own will but the will of the one who sent him in 5:30; his teaching “is not mine, but his who sent me” in 7:16; he does nothing from himself but speaks as the Father taught him in 8:28; and he says in 12:49 that he has not spoken from himself because the Father who sent him gave him a commandment concerning what to say. In John 14:10 Jesus insists that the words he speaks are not spoken independently, while the Father dwelling in him performs the works. This language is not incidental. It constitutes the architecture of Johannine Christology.

## XII. “He Who Has Seen Me Has Seen the Father”: Representation, Not Identity

Seeing Jesus is seeing the Father because the Father’s character, words, works, and authority are manifested through him. John 12:44–45 makes the representational logic explicit: believing in Jesus is ultimately believing in the one who sent him, and seeing Jesus is seeing the one who sent him. The same Gospel that says “whoever has seen me has seen the Father” immediately explains the statement through indwelling and agency. The words are the Father’s words and the works are the Father’s works. Representation therefore explains both sides of John simultaneously: Jesus can speak with staggering divine authority because he is the Father’s unique agent, while he can also repeatedly insist that his authority, teaching, works, judgment, life-giving commission, and mission derive from another.

## XIII. Wisdom Christology in Second Temple Judaism

The Wisdom tradition supplies the second major conceptual framework. Jewish texts could personify God’s Wisdom in highly elevated language without necessarily describing a second coequal deity. Wisdom could be portrayed as existing before creation, participating in God’s works, descending into history, illuminating humanity, reflecting divine glory, and mediating God’s purposes.

## XIV. Personified Wisdom in Proverbs 8

In Proverbs 8 Wisdom is poetically personified as a woman present before or during creation, delighting in God’s creative work and calling humanity toward life. The language is vivid enough that later interpreters debated whether Wisdom is merely poetic personification, a hypostasis, a heavenly intermediary, or something approaching a distinct divine figure. Yet the passage itself arises from wisdom poetry, and nothing requires the conclusion that a second literal divine person stood alongside God as co-Creator.

## XV. Wisdom, Torah, and Historical Embodiment in Sirach 24

Sirach 24 intensifies the imagery. Wisdom speaks in the first person, says she came forth from the mouth of the Most High, dwelt in the heavens, traversed the cosmos, and was commanded by her Creator to pitch her tent in Jacob. The text then associates cosmic Wisdom with the Torah given through Moses. This is crucial. No reader imagines that a Torah scroll was literally a preexistent heavenly woman who consciously traveled through the abyss. Cosmic personification becomes historically localized and embodied in Israel’s covenantal revelation. The movement provides an important analogy for a Wisdom-Christology reading of Jesus: what existed eternally in God as Wisdom, Word, purpose, and self-revelation becomes concretely embodied in history.

## XVI. The Wisdom of Solomon: Image, Radiance, and Divine Activity

The Wisdom of Solomon goes further. Wisdom is described as a holy and intelligent spirit, the fashioner or worker of all things, a pure emanation of God’s glory, a reflection of eternal light, a spotless mirror of God’s activity, and an image of his goodness. These descriptions strongly anticipate language later applied to Christ in Colossians and Hebrews. The conceptual similarity is extremely important. Early Christians already possessed a Jewish theological vocabulary by which cosmic, revelatory, radiant, image-bearing, and creative language could be attributed to God’s Wisdom without requiring a second coequal God.

## XVII. Philo’s Logos and the Spectrum of Jewish Mediatorial Language

Philo of Alexandria demonstrates how flexible such terminology could become. Philo calls the Logos God’s firstborn, image, cosmic mediator, divine reason, and instrument through whom the cosmos is ordered. At times his language concerning the Logos becomes extraordinarily elevated, yet Philo continues to distinguish the supreme God from the Logos and never articulates the later Nicene formula of coequal persons sharing one essence. Scholars disagree over exactly how personally Philo understood the Logos. That ambiguity is itself instructive. Ancient Jewish monotheistic discourse could employ extremely high intermediary language without automatically producing Nicene ontology.

## XVIII. From Eternal Wisdom to Historical Messiah

This becomes the key to understanding early Christian Wisdom Christology. Jesus need not have personally lived as an eternal heavenly individual in order for the New Testament to speak of him using the cosmic biography of Wisdom. If Jesus is understood as the historical embodiment and definitive realization of God’s eternal Wisdom, then what was eternally true of God’s Wisdom can be predicated retrospectively of Christ. The eternal element is God’s Wisdom and purpose; the historical embodiment is Jesus of Nazareth.

## XIX. John 1:1–18: The Word Was God and the Word Became Flesh

John 1:1–18 therefore must be read without assuming in advance that “the Word” is already the consciously existing person Jesus Christ before verse 14. The passage begins, “In the beginning was the Word,” not “In the beginning was Jesus,” “the Messiah,” or even explicitly “the Son.” God’s word in Jewish Scripture is God’s efficacious self-expression. By his word the heavens are made in Psalm 33:6; God speaks creation into existence in Genesis 1; his word accomplishes his purpose in Isaiah 55:10–11. Jewish Wisdom traditions provide an additional bridge by portraying divine Wisdom as present with God, participating in creation, radiant with divine glory, and entering history.

## XX. Was the Logos a Preexistent Person?

The statement “the Word was God” does not necessarily mean that the Word was a second divine person sharing God’s metaphysical essence. John also distinguishes the Logos from “the God” with whom the Logos was. Trinitarian readers reasonably argue that the combination of distinction and deity already points toward a proto-Trinitarian conception. That is a serious interpretation. The Wisdom reading, however, observes that God’s own Wisdom can poetically be “with” God while remaining God’s own self-expression. A person’s word or wisdom can be conceptually distinguished from the person without constituting another independent individual. The decisive development comes in John 1:14: the Word “became flesh.” On the Biblical Unitarian reading, this means God’s eternal Word, Wisdom, purpose, and self-revelation became embodied in the historical man Jesus.

## XXI. John 1:18 and the “Only-Begotten God” Textual Problem

John 1:18 creates a genuine textual and exegetical difficulty. Ancient manuscripts differ between readings equivalent to “unique God” and “unique Son,” and important early witnesses support a form containing *theos*. A fair Unitarian argument should not conceal this evidence. Even if “unique God” is original, however, the verse proves at most that extraordinarily elevated *theos* language can be applied to Jesus. It does not independently settle the meaning of such predication. The same Gospel calls the Father “the only true God” in John 17:3, records the resurrected Jesus speaking of “my God and your God” in John 20:17, and states its purpose as producing belief that Jesus is “the Messiah, the Son of God” in John 20:31.

## XXII. John 8:58: “Before Abraham Was, I Am”

John 8:58 is among the strongest Johannine arguments for personal preexistence and should not be dismissed casually. The temporal contrast between Abraham’s coming into existence and Jesus’ “I am” naturally suggests some kind of existence prior to Abraham. Trinitarian interpretation frequently understands the expression as an echo of divine “I am” language. Yet *ego eimi* itself is not an exclusive divine name; the expression occurs in ordinary Greek, including John 9:9 on the lips of the formerly blind man. The theological question therefore depends on context.

## XXIII. John 17:5 and the Question of Preexistent Glory

John 17:5 likewise presents a major challenge because Jesus asks the Father to glorify him with the glory he had “with” the Father before the world existed. A personal-preexistence reading is straightforward. Yet biblical literature can speak of realities as existing with God in foreknowledge or predetermined purpose before their historical realization. Believers are chosen in Christ before the foundation of the world in Ephesians 1:4; grace is given to believers before the ages in 2 Timothy 1:9; Christ is foreknown before the foundation of the world and manifested later according to 1 Peter 1:20.

## XXIV. Ideal, Prophetic, and Personal Preexistence in Jewish Thought

The Unitarian proposal is therefore not that the New Testament contains no preexistence language. Rather, it distinguishes different kinds of preexistence. Something can preexist personally, poetically, ideally, prophetically, or in divine foreordination. Jewish literature frequently presents realities in heavenly or pretemporal terms without requiring later philosophical conclusions about personal ontology. The question in each passage is what kind of preexistence the author intends.

## XXV. John 10:30: “I and the Father Are One”

John 10:30 states, “I and the Father are one.” Read through later Trinitarian assumptions, this is often paraphrased as “the Father and I are one divine essence.” Yet the Greek adjective is neuter, *hen*, and naturally indicates unity rather than one personal identity. More importantly, John 17 uses closely parallel language when Jesus prays that his disciples may be “one” as he and the Father are one. Whatever divine unity exists between Father and Son is obviously unique in degree, but the linguistic form itself does not demand ontological identity.

## XXVI. Psalm 82 and Jesus’ Own Defense Against the Charge of Deity

The context becomes even more striking when Jesus responds to the accusation that he is making himself God. Instead of explicitly affirming Nicene ontology, Jesus appeals to Psalm 82: “I said, you are gods.” He reasons from lesser figures who could receive divine terminology to himself as the one whom the Father “sanctified and sent into the world,” and he restates his claim as “I am God’s Son.” His defense shows that the biblical category of divinely authorized representatives receiving *god*-language is directly relevant to the controversy.

## XXVII. John 20:28: Thomas’s “My Lord and My God”

Thomas’s confession, “my Lord and my God,” deserves equal candor. The most natural reading is that both titles address Jesus. Attempts to separate the two addresses are grammatically weak. A Biblical Unitarian interpretation is therefore strongest when it accepts the address and asks what “God” means within John’s conceptual world. Psalm 45 could address God’s anointed king as *elohim* while simultaneously speaking of “God, your God.” Moses could be made *elohim* to Pharaoh. God’s representatives could bear divine names and authority. John’s Jesus has just said in 20:17 that the Father is “my God and your God,” and the evangelist shortly afterward says the Gospel was written so readers may believe Jesus is “the Messiah, the Son of God.” Thomas’s confession can therefore be interpreted as the climactic recognition of God uniquely manifested in his risen representative.

## XXVIII. John 17:3: The Father as “The Only True God”

John 17:3 presents a difficulty in the opposite direction. Jesus addresses the Father and calls him “the only true God,” then distinguishes himself as “Jesus Christ whom you have sent.” The ordinary grammatical reading identifies one person, the Father, as the only true God and another person, Jesus, as the Messiah sent by that God. Trinitarian theology can answer that Jesus is speaking of the Father within the relational life of the Trinity and that the same divine nature belongs equally to the Son. That is metaphysically coherent within Trinitarianism, but the sentence itself does not articulate such a qualification.

## XXIX. The Synoptic Jesus: Messiah, Son, and Servant of God

The Synoptic Gospels give even less explicit reason to identify Jesus ontologically as Israel’s God. Mark’s Jesus begins by proclaiming the kingdom “of God,” not his own divine essence. He prays to God, obeys God, trusts God, distinguishes his own will from God’s in Gethsemane, confesses limited knowledge concerning the final hour, and affirms Israel’s confession that God is one.

## XXX. “Why Do You Call Me Good?” Jesus and the One God

Mark 10:18 records Jesus responding to the address “Good Teacher” with the question, “Why do you call me good? No one is good except one, God.” Trinitarian interpreters frequently understand this as a rhetorical challenge rather than a denial of deity, and such an interpretation is possible. Yet an uninitiated reader would naturally hear Jesus distinguishing himself from the one whom he identifies as God.

## XXXI. The Son Who Does Not Know the Hour

Mark 13:32 states that no one knows the day or hour of the consummation, “not even the angels in heaven, nor the Son, but only the Father.” Classical Christology explains this by distinguishing Christ’s human knowledge from his divine omniscience. Yet that two-natures distinction is not stated in the verse. Biblical Unitarianism instead reads the statement according to its natural relational structure: the Father possesses knowledge the Son does not possess.

## XXXII. Jesus and the Shema: The God Whom Jesus Himself Worshiped

In Mark 12:29 Jesus affirms the Shema: “Hear, O Israel: the Lord our God, the Lord is one.” The scribe responds that God “is one, and there is no other besides him,” and Jesus approves his understanding. The God confessed in the dialogue is the God whom Jesus himself worships, obeys, and addresses in prayer. This creates a substantial burden of proof for any interpretation that identifies Jesus ontologically with that same God.

## XXXIII. Forgiving Sins: Divine Prerogative or Delegated Authority?

In Mark 2 Jesus forgives sins, provoking the objection, “Who can forgive sins but God alone?” This is frequently used as a direct argument for deity. Yet Jesus’ conclusion is not “therefore I am God,” but that “the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins.” Matthew’s parallel concludes with the crowd glorifying God “who had given such authority to human beings.” John 20:23 later extends forgiveness-authority to the disciples. The biblical category is therefore compatible with delegated authority.

## XXXIV. The Son of Man, Daniel 7, and Psalm 110

Jesus’ statement that the Son of Man will come with the clouds and sit at God’s right hand combines Daniel 7 and Psalm 110. Yet Daniel’s humanlike figure receives authority from the Ancient of Days rather than being the Ancient of Days. Psalm 110 similarly distinguishes YHWH from the royal lord seated beside him. The imagery is one of extraordinary exaltation alongside God, not necessarily identification as the God beside whom the Messiah sits.

## XXXV. Matthew 28:18–20: Given Authority and the Baptismal Formula

Matthew 28:18 records Jesus saying, “all authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me.” The passive construction matters. Authority is conferred by another. The triadic baptismal formula of Matthew 28:19 associates Father, Son, and Holy Spirit in a profoundly significant manner, but association itself does not define ontology. The text does not state that the three are coequal persons sharing one essence.

## XXXVI. Luke 1:35: The Origin and Meaning of Jesus’ Divine Sonship

Luke’s infancy narrative supplies an explicit reason Jesus will be called the Son of God. Gabriel tells Mary that the Holy Spirit will come upon her and the power of the Most High will overshadow her; “therefore” the holy child to be born will be called Son of God. Nothing in the immediate explanation describes an eternally existing Son entering Mary’s womb. The narrative moves from miraculous divine causation to sonship.

## XXXVII. Acts and the Apostolic Jesus: “A Man Attested by God”

Acts is still more explicit. Peter calls Jesus “a man attested to you by God with mighty works and wonders and signs that God did through him” in Acts 2:22. The distinction is remarkably direct: Jesus is the man; God is the one performing works through him. God raises him from the dead, exalts him, and makes him Lord and Messiah.

## XXXVIII. “God Was With Him”: Peter’s Christology in Acts 10:38

Acts 10:38 summarizes Jesus’ ministry with extraordinary clarity: “God anointed Jesus of Nazareth with the Holy Spirit and with power,” and Jesus healed because “God was with him.” The sentence distinguishes the anointer from the anointed and explains Jesus’ miraculous ministry through divine empowerment. Trinitarianism can integrate this into incarnation theology, but the text itself naturally reads as agency Christology.

## XXXIX. Resurrection and Exaltation: God Makes Jesus Lord and Messiah

Acts repeatedly describes Jesus’ post-resurrection status as something God grants. God raises him, glorifies him, places him at his right hand, makes him Lord and Messiah, and appoints him as judge. These verbs have real theological force. Biblical Unitarianism treats exaltation as an actual transition in status rather than merely the public unveiling of an eternal rank Jesus always possessed intrinsically.

## XL. Paul’s Adam Christology: The Obedient Human Messiah

Paul constructs major portions of his Christology around Jesus as the counterpart to Adam. The parallel is fundamentally anthropological. Through one man came sin and death; through another man come righteousness, resurrection, and life. Jesus succeeds precisely where the first human being failed.

## XLI. Romans 5: Jesus as the Last and Successful Adam

Romans 5 repeatedly describes the saving achievement of “the one man Jesus Christ.” Through Adam’s disobedience many were made sinners; through the obedience of the corresponding righteous man many are constituted righteous. The argument derives theological power from Jesus’ representative humanity.

## XLII. First Corinthians 15: Resurrection Through a Man

First Corinthians 15:21 states, “since death came through a man, resurrection of the dead has also come through a man.” Christ is the last Adam and the firstfruits of resurrected humanity. The gospel’s victory over death therefore depends upon God bringing resurrection through a genuine human representative.

## XLIII. First Corinthians 8:6 and the One God, the Father

First Corinthians 8:6 states, “for us there is one God, the Father, from whom are all things and for whom we exist, and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things and through whom we exist.” The text explicitly identifies the “one God” as the Father and distinguishes Jesus as the “one Lord.”

## XLIV. Does Paul “Include Jesus in the Shema”?

A major modern Trinitarian argument maintains that Paul is reformulating Israel’s Shema by placing “God” language upon the Father and “Lord” language upon Jesus, thereby including Christ within the unique divine identity. This is one of the strongest non-Nicene arguments for Jesus’ deity because it attempts to remain within first-century Jewish categories rather than importing later substance metaphysics. Yet the argument remains debated. The grammatical fact is that Paul says “one God, the Father” and separately “one Lord, Jesus Christ.” A representational reading understands all things as ultimately originating “from” God and operating “through” the Lord whom God appoints.

## XLV. Philippians 2:5–11: Incarnation or Adamic Exaltation?

Philippians 2:5–11 is another major battleground. Traditional preexistence Christology reads the passage as the descent of an eternal divine person who existed in God’s form, refrained from exploiting equality, assumed humanity, died, and returned to divine glory. An alternative Adamic interpretation sees Jesus as the true human image of God who, unlike Adam, refuses to grasp at equality with God, assumes the role of a servant, obeys to death, and is consequently exalted.

## XLVI. “Form of God,” Equality With God, and the Meaning of *Harpagmos*

The phrase “form of God” need not automatically mean possession of the metaphysical essence of deity. In an Adamic and image-of-God context, the language can signify divine image, status, or representation. The disputed term *harpagmos* concerns how Christ relates to equality with God: something not exploited, not grasped, or not treated as an advantage. The debate is genuine, and the passage cannot responsibly be reduced to a single lexical slogan.

## XLVII. The Name Above Every Name and the Application of Isaiah 45

Philippians 2 applies Isaiah 45’s universal bowing language to Jesus. This is serious evidence for extraordinarily high Christology. Yet the text explicitly says God exalted Jesus and “gave” him the name above every name. Every tongue confesses Jesus as Lord “to the glory of God the Father.” The honor is mediated through the Messiah and terminates in the glory of God who exalted him.

## XLVIII. Colossians 1:15–20 and the Image of the Invisible God

Colossians calls Christ “the image of the invisible God.” The terminology itself is representational. An image manifests someone other than itself. Adam was created in God’s image; Christ is the perfected and definitive image in whom God’s purpose for humanity reaches fulfillment.

## XLIX. “Firstborn of All Creation”: Creation, Kingship, and Supremacy

“Firstborn of all creation” does not necessarily mean “first creature,” but neither does it inherently mean “eternally generated divine person.” In Psalm 89 the Davidic king can be made God’s “firstborn, the highest of the kings of the earth.” Firstborn terminology can therefore indicate supremacy, inheritance, and royal status.

## L. Were All Things Literally Created Through a Preexistent Jesus?

The phrase that all things were created “through” Christ presents real pressure toward personal preexistence. An exclusively “new creation” interpretation struggles with references to heavens, earth, visible and invisible realities, thrones, dominions, rulers, and authorities. A stronger Wisdom-Christology reading accepts the cosmic reference while asking whether the conscious human Jesus himself must have been personally present at Genesis.

## LI. Cosmic Creation Language Through the Lens of Wisdom Christology

Jewish Wisdom traditions already attributed cosmic creative participation to God’s Wisdom. If the historical Jesus becomes the definitive embodiment of that Wisdom, the cosmic biography of Wisdom can become christologically predicated of him. In this reading Christ is not necessarily a second craftsman standing beside God at creation; the Wisdom through which God created is now personally realized in the Messiah.

## LII. Hebrews 1: Radiance, Image, Sonship, and Exaltation

Hebrews describes the Son as the radiance of God’s glory and the exact imprint of God’s reality, terms strikingly reminiscent of Wisdom of Solomon. The Son is heir of all things, God’s climactic revealer, and the exalted ruler seated at the right hand of Majesty.

## LIII. Psalm 45: “Your Throne, O God” and “God, Your God”

Hebrews applies Psalm 45 to Jesus: “Your throne, O God, is forever and ever.” Yet the quotation continues, “therefore God, your God, has anointed you.” The original psalm concerns a royal figure who can receive *elohim*-language while remaining subordinate to the God who anoints him. Hebrews’ reuse of the text therefore does not automatically transform “God” into a Nicene ontological category.

## LIV. Hebrews 1:10–12: The Strongest Creator-Christology Objection

Hebrews 1:10–12 is one of the most difficult passages for a non-preexistent-person interpretation. The author quotes Psalm 102’s language concerning the Lord who founded the earth and created the heavens, and most interpreters understand the quotation as addressed to the Son. If so, a passage originally concerning YHWH the Creator is directly applied to Christ. An honest Unitarian reading should acknowledge that this constitutes substantial counterevidence. Wisdom Christology and representational transfer offer possible explanations, but the conventional reading gives advocates of personal preexistence a genuinely strong argument.

## LV. Romans 9:5: Is Christ “God Over All”?

Romans 9:5 may describe Christ as “God over all, blessed forever,” though punctuation and syntactical possibilities have generated longstanding debate. A fair analysis should not claim the christological reading is grammatically impossible. Even if Paul here calls Christ *theos*, however, the broader question remains what such divine predication means in a Jewish representational environment where subordinate figures can receive divine terminology.

## LVI. Titus 2:13 and 2 Peter 1:1: “God and Savior”

Titus 2:13 and 2 Peter 1:1 very naturally permit, and perhaps favor, applying the combined title “God and Savior” to Jesus. These texts should be conceded as significant evidence. Yet calling Jesus *theos* is not itself identical with saying that Jesus is one of three coequal persons sharing one divine essence. That latter proposition remains a theological interpretation of the title.

## LVII. Revelation: The Lamb, the Throne, and Heavenly Worship

Revelation gives Jesus one of the highest statuses imaginable. The Lamb receives cosmic praise, exercises divine judgment, participates in heavenly rule, shares the throne, and bears extraordinary titles. This cannot reasonably be reduced to the status of an ordinary prophet.

## LVIII. “My God” After the Resurrection: Revelation 3:12

Yet Revelation also contains a remarkable counterweight. The exalted Jesus repeatedly refers to the Father as “my God” in Revelation 3:12. This occurs not during earthly humiliation but after resurrection and heavenly exaltation. The relational distinction between Jesus and his God therefore survives glorification.

## LIX. Early Christian Worship of Jesus and the Evidence of High Christology

The earliest Christian movement undeniably exhibited extraordinarily elevated devotion to Jesus. Christians prayed and acted in his name, invoked him, baptized with reference to him, celebrated the Lord’s Supper around him, sang hymnic material concerning him, confessed him as Lord, and expected his return as universal ruler. Any historical reconstruction in which Jesus remained an insignificant teacher until Constantine suddenly declared him divine is demonstrably inadequate.

## LX. Does Worship Prove Ontological Deity?

Yet devotional elevation does not automatically settle ontology. A God-authorized ruler may receive extraordinary homage precisely because God commands humanity to honor his representative. Philippians 2 presents universal confession of Jesus’ lordship as the consequence of God’s act of exaltation and states that this confession culminates in the glory of God the Father.

## LXI. Divine Prerogatives and the Logic of Delegated Authority

Trinitarian arguments frequently reason that God alone forgives sins, judges the world, saves humanity, gives life, and receives worship; Jesus does these things; therefore Jesus is God. The hidden premise is that possession of a divine prerogative necessarily proves ontological identity with God. Yet Scripture repeatedly shows God delegating functions that ultimately belong to him.

## LXII. Can Only God Forgive, Judge, Save, and Give Life?

God empowers judges to save Israel, priests to mediate forgiveness, prophets to speak his words, kings to execute judgment, apostles to exercise delegated authority, and messengers to bear his name. John 5 explicitly says the Father “gave” Jesus authority to judge. Acts 17 says God will judge the world through a man whom he appointed. Matthew 28 says universal authority was given to Jesus. Delegated possession of divine authority is therefore a biblical category rather than an ad hoc Unitarian invention.

## LXIII. *Proskyneō*: Worship, Homage, and Royal Prostration

The Greek verb *proskyneō* can describe worship directed to God, but it can also describe prostration, homage, or obeisance before human superiors and rulers. Biblical people bow before kings without believing those kings to be YHWH. Consequently the mere fact that individuals *proskyneō* Jesus cannot independently prove his ontological deity. Context must determine the nature of the honor.

## LXIV. YHWH Texts Applied to Jesus: Identity or Representation?

Several New Testament texts originally referring to YHWH are applied to Jesus. This constitutes an important argument for high Christology and must not be minimized. The issue is whether scriptural transfer necessarily implies ontological identity. Biblical patterns of representation, name-bearing, royal enthronement, Wisdom embodiment, and delegated divine activity provide another conceptual possibility.

## LXV. Exodus 23 and Bearing the Divine Name

Exodus 23 is particularly significant because God’s angel bears God’s “name” and therefore carries extraordinary authority. To bear the divine name is not necessarily to be the God whose name is borne. If such a category exists within Israel’s Scriptures, then the Messiah’s reception of God’s supreme name or titles need not automatically collapse agent into principal.

## LXVI. Jesus as “Lord”: Divine Name or Messianic Office?

The Greek *kyrios* can represent the divine name in the Septuagint, but it also means master, owner, ruler, or lord. Its application to Jesus must therefore be evaluated contextually. Acts 2:36 says God “made” Jesus both Lord and Messiah, strongly suggesting conferred lordship. In many New Testament passages “God” and “the Lord Jesus” function as distinguishable titles.

## LXVII. Jesus as “Savior”: Source and Agent of Salvation

God is Israel’s ultimate Savior, yet Scripture can also designate human deliverers as saviors because God saves through them. Jesus’ being Savior therefore need not mean that he is ontologically identical with the God who saves through him. God is the ultimate source; Christ is the appointed mediator through whom salvation reaches humanity.

## LXVIII. “Son of God”: Biblical Meaning Versus Later Metaphysical Meaning

“Son of God” does not lexically mean “God the Son, second person of a triune essence.” Israel is called God’s son; the Davidic king is God’s son; Adam is called God’s son in Luke’s genealogy; righteous people can be called children of God. Jesus’ sonship is unique and supreme, but the biblical title arises from royal, messianic, covenantal, and miraculous-conception categories rather than from the vocabulary of later metaphysics.

## LXIX. Jesus Has a God: The Persistent New Testament Testimony

The repeated assertion that Jesus has a God presents one of the simplest challenges to ontological equality. On the cross Jesus cries, “My God, my God.” After resurrection he says, “I ascend to my Father and your Father, to my God and your God.” Paul speaks of “the God of our Lord Jesus Christ.” Peter blesses “the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ.” Revelation’s glorified Jesus continues saying “my God.” Trinitarian theology explains these expressions through incarnation and relational distinctions, but the texts themselves do not append the qualification “according to his human nature.”

## LXX. First Corinthians 15:24–28 and the Son’s Final Subjection

First Corinthians 15:24–28 is especially important because it concerns the consummation after Christ’s resurrection and universal reign. Christ hands the kingdom to “God the Father.” God subjects everything to Christ while being explicitly excluded from the things subjected. Then “the Son himself will also be subjected to the one who subjected all things to him, so that God may be all in all.” The hierarchy therefore extends beyond earthly humiliation into the final order of redemption.

## LXXI. First Timothy 2:5: The One God and the Human Mediator

First Timothy 2:5 gives the structure with remarkable economy: “there is one God, and one mediator between God and humanity, the human Christ Jesus.” A mediator is conceptually distinguishable from the party toward whom he mediates. The text identifies the one God and then identifies the mediator as a human being. Trinitarian theology can supplement this with two-natures Christology, but the supplementation is not stated in the sentence itself.

## LXXII. Two-Natures Christology: Explanation or Imported Assumption?

The doctrine that Jesus possesses a complete divine nature and a complete human nature united in one person became the classical Christian solution to the biblical tension between divine and human language. It is philosophically sophisticated and capable of reconciling many difficult texts. The methodological question is whether the New Testament itself requires this ontology or whether it emerged later as one theological way of harmonizing texts that originally functioned within Jewish categories of agency, Wisdom, royal representation, and exaltation.

## LXXIII. Comparing the Explanatory Models: Trinitarianism and Biblical Unitarianism

The Trinitarian model explains the highest texts by taking their strongest apparent implications ontologically: the Word is God; Jesus predates Abraham; Christ participates in creation; YHWH texts are applied to him; Thomas calls him God; he receives worship; Hebrews applies Creator language to him. It then explains Jesus’ dependency by distinguishing divine and human natures, eternal and economic relations, functional obedience, incarnation, and voluntary humiliation. The Biblical Unitarian model reverses the explanatory priority. It begins with the explicit identity and relational statements: the Father is “the only true God”; “for us there is one God, the Father”; Jesus is “the man Christ Jesus”; God works through him, raises him, anoints him, gives him authority, exalts him, makes him Lord, appoints him judge, and remains his God.

## LXXIV. Ontological Identity Versus Functional and Representational Identity

The central logical distinction is between being someone and representing someone. Scripture can predicate the sender’s words, authority, throne, judgment, saving activity, titles, and even name of an authorized agent without making the agent ontologically identical with the sender. Functional identity concerns what the agent does and whom he represents; ontological identity concerns what the agent is. The existence of the former does not logically entail the latter.

## LXXV. The Principle of Explanatory Economy

On ordinary historical-grammatical grounds, the Biblical Unitarian model possesses an important advantage of explanatory economy. When one person repeatedly calls another “my God,” calls him “the only true God,” says he was sent by him, taught by him, commanded by him, empowered by him, heard by him in prayer, raised by him, exalted by him, and given authority by him, the natural conclusion is that the first person is not the God to whom he stands in all those relations. One can construct a metaphysical system in which both remain equally divine, but that metaphysical distinction constitutes an additional interpretive layer.

## LXXVI. The Strongest Trinitarian Counterarguments

A responsible Biblical Unitarian case must concede that John 1, John 8:58, John 17:5, John 20:28, Philippians 2, Colossians 1, Hebrews 1, Titus 2:13, 2 Peter 1:1, YHWH texts applied to Christ, and early devotional practices constitute significant evidence for very high Christology. These passages cannot simply be dismissed as mistranslations or corruptions without argument.

## LXXVII. The Strongest Biblical Unitarian Responses

The Unitarian response is cumulative rather than dependent upon one lexical escape. God-language can be representational; Wisdom can preexist without being a second coequal person; messianic glory can exist in divine foreordination; YHWH texts can be transferred to God’s supreme representative; authority and worship can be divinely authorized; and the New Testament repeatedly distinguishes Jesus from the God who grants him everything he possesses.

## LXXVIII. Where the Biblical Unitarian Case Must Remain Cautious

The strongest Unitarian position should therefore avoid claiming that every passage is effortless. Hebrews 1:10–12 and John 17:5 remain particularly challenging. Colossians 1 and 1 Corinthians 8:6 require serious treatment of cosmic creation language. Thomas’s confession must be accepted rather than grammatically evaded. Early Christ devotion must be acknowledged as genuinely extraordinary. Intellectual honesty strengthens rather than weakens the argument.

## LXXIX. High Christology Without Ontological Deity

The fundamental false dilemma is the assumption that Jesus must either be an ordinary human teacher or the ontological God of Israel. Second Temple Jewish categories provide many intermediate conceptual possibilities: supreme agent, heavenly Messiah, Son of Man, Wisdom embodiment, divine image, name-bearer, enthroned vice-regent, eschatological judge, and universal Lord. High Christology does not logically require Nicene ontology.

## LXXX. Jesus as the Perfect Image of God

Second Corinthians 4:4 calls Christ the image of God; Colossians 1:15 calls him the image of the invisible God; Hebrews describes him as the imprint of God’s reality. An image is not the original object but its visible representation. Jesus is therefore not diminished by being God’s perfect image. His greatness lies precisely in manifesting the invisible God without ceasing to be the human being through whom God is revealed.

## LXXXI. The Reality of Jesus’ Human Obedience, Faith, and Temptation

The New Testament repeatedly emphasizes Jesus’ obedience, temptation, dependence, prayer, growth, suffering, and trust. Hebrews says he learned obedience through what he suffered. The Gospels portray his temptation as real. He receives the Spirit, prays for help, entrusts himself to God, and acts through divine empowerment. Within Biblical Unitarianism these are straightforward descriptions of genuine human faithfulness.

## LXXXII. Why Genuine Humanity Matters for Redemption

If humanity fell through a human representative, redemption appropriately comes through a faithful human representative. Jesus does not save by bypassing humanity but by fulfilling humanity’s vocation. He becomes the obedient Son Adam failed to be, the faithful Israelite Israel repeatedly failed to be, and the righteous Davidic king Israel’s monarchs failed to be.

## LXXXIII. Jesus the Second Adam and the Restoration of Humanity

Paul’s Adam-Christ parallel therefore reaches its full theological force when Jesus is understood as genuinely human. Through Adam’s disobedience humanity falls under death; through Christ’s obedience humanity receives resurrection and life. Jesus becomes the beginning of renewed humanity, not the replacement of humanity by deity.

## LXXXIV. Resurrection as Vindication and Exaltation

The resurrection is God’s vindication of Jesus. God raises the one whom human authorities crucified, overturning their judgment and publicly declaring him righteous. The resurrection then leads to exaltation, enthronement, lordship, and universal authority.

## LXXXV. Was Jesus Restored to an Eternal Status or Newly Exalted?

Classical incarnation theology often reads exaltation as the incarnate Son’s return to or manifestation of the glory that eternally belonged to him. The apostolic speeches in Acts, however, present exaltation as something God actually does to Jesus: God makes him Lord, raises him, glorifies him, and gives him authority. Biblical Unitarianism takes these statements as real changes in the Messiah’s historical status.

## LXXXVI. From Jewish Agency to Nicene Ontology: The Historical Development

The rise of ontological Christology becomes historically intelligible once these trajectories are recognized. Christians applied Wisdom language personally to Jesus, reread Israel’s Scriptures christologically, prayed in Jesus’ name, confessed him as universal Lord, and placed him beside God’s throne. As Christianity increasingly entered Greek intellectual environments, questions concerning essence, nature, substance, generation, and personhood became more explicit.

## LXXXVII. Nicaea, Constantinople, and the Metaphysical Reinterpretation of Christ

The fourth-century councils did not invent Christian devotion to Jesus. They attempted to define metaphysically how the exalted Son relates to the Father. The Unitarian critique is therefore not that Constantine suddenly turned an ordinary Galilean into God. Rather, it is that biblical categories of agency, Wisdom, image, sonship, and exaltation were increasingly interpreted through ontological categories foreign to their original Jewish literary environment.

## LXXXVIII. Why High Christology Does Not Necessarily Equal Trinitarianism

One can acknowledge that the earliest Christians held an extraordinarily high view of Jesus without concluding that they confessed the later doctrine of one essence in three coequal persons. Early devotion demonstrates exaltation; it does not by itself specify metaphysical ontology. The theological leap from “Jesus shares God’s authority and receives God-authorized devotion” to “Jesus eternally possesses the identical divine essence as the Father” requires additional argument.

## LXXXIX. Reconstructing the Biblical Christological Framework

When the biblical categories are allowed to operate in their own historical environment, the picture becomes coherent. The one God commissions agents, embodies his Wisdom in revelation, places his name upon representatives, installs kings upon his throne, grants authority to judges and prophets, and ultimately appoints one human Messiah above every other agent.

## XC. God → Messiah → Humanity: The New Testament’s Relational Structure

The dominant relational architecture can therefore be summarized as God → Messiah → humanity and creation. God is the ultimate source; Jesus is the supreme mediator. God sends; Jesus is sent. God authorizes; Jesus exercises the authority. God raises; Jesus is raised. God exalts; Jesus is exalted. God gives revelation; Jesus reveals it. Humanity comes to God through the Messiah whom God appointed.

## XCI. The Eternal Wisdom of God Embodied in the Historical Jesus

Jesus’ uniqueness nevertheless surpasses ordinary agency because he is the definitive embodiment of God’s Wisdom. The Wisdom through which God conceived and ordered creation becomes historically visible in the Messiah. The divine image obscured in Adam is perfected in him. The Davidic kingship reaches universality in him. Prophetic revelation reaches its climax in him. God’s purpose for humanity becomes embodied in one faithful person.

## XCII. The Word Becoming Flesh as Fulfillment Rather Than Metamorphosis

From this perspective, “the Word became flesh” is better understood as fulfillment than as the metamorphosis of one divine person into a human nature. God’s Word, Wisdom, promise, self-expression, and redemptive purpose become historical, visible, tangible, and personal in Jesus. The incarnation remains profound, but its meaning becomes the embodiment of divine revelation rather than necessarily the enfleshment of a second eternal divine person.

## XCIII. Jesus as God’s Supreme *Shaliaḥ*

The category of *shaliaḥ*, carefully freed from anachronistic dependence upon later rabbinic codification, provides a powerful conceptual analogy. Jesus is the supreme sent one. He speaks God’s words, performs God’s works, bears God’s authority, reveals God’s character, acts in God’s name, and executes God’s judgment. His representation is so complete that responding to Jesus constitutes responding to God.

## XCIV. The Biblical Meaning of Seeing, Hearing, and Receiving God Through Jesus

To hear the Son is to hear the Father because the Father speaks through him. To reject the Son is to reject the Father because the Father sent him. To honor the Son is to honor the Father because God appointed him. To see Jesus is to see what the invisible God is like. None of this requires that representative and represented be numerically the same being. Indeed, representation presupposes distinction.

## XCV. “God Was in Christ”: Presence Without Ontological Identity

Second Corinthians 5:19 captures this structure succinctly: “God was in Christ reconciling the world to himself.” The sentence distinguishes God, Christ, and the world being reconciled. God is the ultimate reconciler; Christ is the one in and through whom reconciliation occurs. Divine presence in Christ is therefore compatible with personal distinction and agency.

## XCVI. Reintroducing the Biblical Jesus

The biblical Jesus who emerges is neither the abstract second person of a later metaphysical formulation nor a merely ethical Galilean teacher. He is Jesus of Nazareth, the Messiah of Israel and Son of the living God, the human being through whom Israel’s God acts decisively for the world. He is uniquely conceived by God’s Spirit, perfectly obedient, the definitive prophet, Davidic king, suffering servant, high priest, embodiment of divine Wisdom, image of the invisible God, eschatological Son of Man, firstborn from the dead, ruler of the new creation, and appointed judge of humanity.

## XCVII. The True Mission of Jesus Christ

Jesus’ mission is to reveal his Father, announce and inaugurate God’s kingdom, call humanity to repentance and covenant faithfulness, embody divine compassion and righteousness, defeat sin through obedience, surrender his life in faithfulness, be vindicated through resurrection, receive universal authority from God, gather the renewed people of God, mediate the new covenant, and ultimately bring creation into submission to God.

## XCVIII. Recovering the Apostolic Gospel of the Kingdom

The earliest apostolic preaching focuses primarily upon these events rather than upon metaphysical definitions of divine essence. Peter proclaims Jesus as the man accredited by God, crucified by human beings, raised by God, exalted to God’s right hand, and made Lord and Messiah. Paul proclaims the descendant of David raised from death and established in power. First Corinthians 15 centers upon Christ’s death, burial, resurrection, appearances, and future victory. The original gospel is resurrection-centered, kingdom-centered, messianic, and eschatological.

## XCIX. Theological Implications of a Corrected Christology

A corrected Christology changes several theological emphases. Faith becomes faith in the God whom Jesus trusted and in the Messiah whom that God raised. Worship remains centered upon the one God, the Father, while Jesus receives the supreme messianic honor God commands. Discipleship becomes imitation of a genuinely human Son whose obedience was real rather than merely apparent. Resurrection becomes the vindication and transformation of humanity. Mediation becomes conceptually meaningful because the mediator genuinely stands between God and humanity. Jesus’ humanity becomes not an obstacle to high Christology but the very reason his achievement matters for human salvation.

## C. Conclusion: Not God the Son, but the Son of God

Traditional Trinitarianism deserves to be treated as a serious and historically sophisticated attempt to reconcile the New Testament’s highest statements about Christ with biblical monotheism. It cannot responsibly be dismissed with slogans, and texts such as John 1, John 8:58, John 17:5, John 20:28, Philippians 2, Colossians 1, Hebrews 1, Titus 2:13, 2 Peter 1:1, the application of YHWH passages to Jesus, and early Christian devotion remain genuine challenges for Biblical Unitarian interpretation. Nevertheless, when the complete biblical pattern is weighed, the Unitarian synthesis possesses considerable explanatory strength. It permits explicit distinctions between God and Jesus to retain their ordinary meaning; it takes Jesus’ humanity seriously without requiring unstated divisions between divine and human consciousness; it explains divine titles through scripturally established representation; it explains cosmic language through Wisdom, Word, and divine foreordination; it explains exalted honor through God-authorized messianic lordship; and it situates later ontological Christology within an intelligible historical development.

The decisive biblical question is therefore not whether Jesus can be spoken of in divine categories. Clearly he can. The question is why. Traditional Trinitarianism answers that Jesus can bear divine language because he is ontologically the eternal God the Son. Biblical Unitarianism answers that Jesus can bear such language because Israel’s one God has uniquely embodied his Wisdom in him, placed his authority upon him, made him his perfect image, given him his name and prerogatives, raised him from death, seated him at his right hand, and appointed him supreme representative over creation.

When the clearest relational statements are allowed to govern the more symbolic and exalted ones, the resulting picture is remarkably coherent. The Father is the one whom Jesus calls “the only true God.” The Father is “the one God” from whom all things originate. Jesus is the Messiah sent by that God. He is the man through whom God performs mighty works, the Son whom God raises, the Lord whom God exalts, the judge whom God appoints, the mediator between God and humanity, and the ruler who ultimately subjects himself to God so that God may be all in all.

Calling Jesus “God’s Man” therefore need not diminish him. Biblically understood, God’s Man is the one through whom God has chosen to rule the world. He is greater than Moses because he embodies the revelation Moses anticipated; greater than David because his kingdom is everlasting; greater than Solomon because he embodies divine Wisdom; greater than angels because God has enthroned him above them; greater than every priest because he is the resurrected high priest; and greater than Adam because he inaugurates a new humanity. Yet at every point his greatness remains relational: God sends, empowers, raises, exalts, commissions, and glorifies him.

The most historically and logically economical conclusion is therefore that the New Testament’s dominant framework is not “God became his own human agent,” but “God acted uniquely and definitively through the human Messiah whom he appointed.” Jesus is not an ordinary human being subsequently exaggerated into deity. Neither must he be identified ontologically with the God whom he calls Father and God. He is the supreme Son, agent, image, Wisdom-bearer, Messiah, and Lord through whom the one God accomplishes his redemptive purpose.

To reintroduce the biblical Jesus is therefore to return to the apostolic direction of the gospel. The God of Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and Israel has acted decisively through Jesus of Nazareth. God’s kingdom has drawn near through his Messiah. Jesus proclaimed repentance, righteousness, mercy, resurrection, judgment, and the coming kingdom. He embodied God’s character in his works, obeyed faithfully even unto death, and was vindicated when God raised him. God then exalted him, seated him at his right hand, placed all created powers beneath him, and appointed him the mediator through whom forgiveness, resurrection, the Spirit, and the coming age are offered to humanity.

The Christian confession can therefore retain its breathtaking height without confusing the Messiah with the God who sent him: there is one God, the Father, and one Lord, Jesus Christ. The Father is the ultimate source of creation and salvation; Jesus is the supreme agent through whom God’s purposes are accomplished. The Son perfectly reveals the Father without becoming the Father or replacing him. The image manifests the invisible God without becoming the archetype. The Messiah exercises divine authority because God has entrusted it to him. The Word of God becomes flesh because God’s eternal Wisdom and redemptive purpose reach their definitive human embodiment in Jesus.

Thus, between the alternatives “God-Man” and “God’s Man,” the latter—properly understood in the categories of biblical agency, messianic kingship, Wisdom Christology, exaltation, and Jewish monotheism—offers a historically grounded and logically coherent explanation of the entire New Testament witness. Jesus is not “merely” a man. He is the man whom God has made the center of his redemptive purpose: the true Adam, the faithful Israelite, the Davidic Messiah, the definitive prophet, the resurrected Son of Man, the embodiment of divine Wisdom, the perfect image of God, the mediator of the new covenant, and the universal Lord appointed by the one God.

The original gospel consequently directs humanity through Jesus to the God whom Jesus himself worshiped. God raised Jesus from the dead. God made him Lord and Messiah. God placed him at his right hand. God will judge the world through the man he appointed. God will raise those who belong to Christ. And after every enemy has been defeated, the Son himself will remain subject to the God who subjected all things to him, “so that God may be all in all.”

The biblical Jesus is therefore not less glorious because he is the Son of God rather than “God the Son.” His glory is precisely that a genuine human being has perfectly fulfilled God’s purpose for humanity and has consequently been exalted above every other created authority. In him humanity’s intended destiny is finally revealed. Through him God reconciles the world. Through him God grants resurrection. Through him God rules the coming age. Through him believers come to the Father.

The great christological confession is consequently not that the distinction between God and his Messiah must be dissolved, but that the distinction has become the means of salvation: “God was in Christ reconciling the world to himself.” God is the reconciler; Christ is his definitive human mediator; humanity is the object of reconciliation. The gospel announces that the one God has acted through the one man whom he raised from death and exalted as Lord. To believe that gospel is to acknowledge Jesus as God’s Messiah, submit to the authority God has given him, follow him as the true human image of God, share in his resurrection hope, and through him come to know the Father whom Jesus himself called “the only true God.”
